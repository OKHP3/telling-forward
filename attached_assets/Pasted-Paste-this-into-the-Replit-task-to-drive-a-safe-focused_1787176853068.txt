# Telling Forward: Stage 0–1 Product Build Directive

## Your role

Act as the lead product engineer and product designer for **Telling Forward**, an OverKill Hill P³ project.

First inspect the existing Replit workspace and repository. Read `AGENTS.md`, `README.md`, `CONTRIBUTING.md`, `CONTENT-LICENSE.md`, `docs/MISSION.md`, `docs/platform-requirements.md`, `docs/product/dream-platform-specification.md`, `docs/product/attainable-delivery-roadmap.md`, the architecture decision records under `docs/adr/`, and `docs/decisions/open-questions.md`.

Treat these sources as the project’s current evidence base. Preserve existing work. Do not replace the application with a generic starter, delete unrelated files, invent policy decisions, or treat a draft requirement as an implemented feature.

Your job is to turn the project’s present concept and scaffold into a coherent, working **Stage 0–1 pilot**. Build the first valuable, safe version of the product, not the entire dream platform.

## Product identity

Telling Forward is a voice-first, agent-assisted collaborative storytelling platform for **open-canon collaborative fiction**.

It helps people turn memories, ideas, characters, story arcs, and fragments into readable, attributable story material. An originating author or steward can open a storyworld, invite bounded participation, and distinguish the official canon from alternate continuities.

The product’s central promise is:

> Make storytelling more accessible without making authorship, consent, attribution, or creative governance unclear.

The reader is the front door. The writer’s private creative process is the foundation. Collaboration is an opt-in layer, not a prerequisite for value.

## The pilot outcome

Build a private-first Writer’s Workbench and a basic Storyworld Reader for one demonstrably owned or synthetic pilot world.

At the end of the pilot, one worldbuilder should be able to:

1. Create and organize original story ideas as small, reviewable capsules.
2. Record clear authorship, visibility, permission context, and source provenance for each capsule.
3. Shape selected capsules into a simple story seed or scene draft through intentional manual actions.
4. Create or configure one structured storyworld.
5. Publish a basic reader-safe edition of selected, approved story content.
6. See canon, alternate, draft, and private material clearly distinguished.
7. Update a scene and republish a reader edition without needing to understand Git.

The pilot must use manual capture and owned or synthetic fixtures only. Do not imply that untrusted uploads, public submissions, commercial rights, or automatic AI processing are ready.

## Product principles

Every implementation and interface decision must respect these principles:

1. Human intent stays in charge. Agents may suggest, organize, and flag. Humans approve represented text and humans make canon decisions.
2. Readers come first. A branching world must remain pleasant and coherent to read.
3. Private before public. Work begins private unless a human deliberately changes its visibility.
4. Canon is not possibility. Alternate paths are meaningful but never presented as official canon.
5. Provenance is durable. Important content and decisions retain a visible relationship to origin, contributor, permission, and revision.
6. Consent is specific. A generic “processing allowed” flag is not permission for publication, derivative use, AI context, translation, commercial evaluation, or adaptation.
7. Git stays backstage. Use friendly storytelling language in the product interface. Technical implementation may use Git and GitHub terminology internally.
8. Accessible creation matters. Do not assume that users identify as writers, know version control, or feel comfortable beginning with a blank page.
9. Safety is a feature. Do not add public contribution, real external uploads, or rights-sensitive transformations before their required safety controls exist.
10. Explain only what helps. Keep the interface calm, clear, and respectful.

## Required product vocabulary

Never expose raw Git or GitHub vocabulary to ordinary readers and contributors.

Use these public terms:

| Technical concept | Product language |
| --- | --- |
| Repository | Storyworld |
| Branch | Story path |
| Commit | Saved moment |
| Pull request | Submit your scene |
| Merge | Accepted into official story |
| Issue or comment | Story note or editor feedback |

Use these contribution outcomes distinctly:

- Personal work
- Open path
- Proposed canon
- Published alternate path

Use this contributor-friendly editorial state model when the product needs to show status:

- Draft
- Submitted
- Under review
- Returned with notes
- Accepted into canon
- Published as an alternate path

For this Stage 0–1 pilot, private drafting and a static or controlled publication flow are sufficient. Do not pretend the full contributor review loop is complete if it is not.

## In-scope build

### 1. Worldbuilder home

Create a calm, capable home for a worldbuilder.

It should show:

- Their storyworld or pilot world
- A clear current stage, such as “private workshop” or “reader edition published”
- A focused checklist for the pilot
- Recent saved moments
- A clear entry point to the Writer’s Workbench
- A clear entry point to preview or publish the Reader edition
- A visible reminder that private work is private by default

Do not make this a business dashboard. It should feel more like a creative studio and library than a project-management tool.

### 2. Private Writer’s Workbench

Build a private-first Concept Board where a writer can manually capture and organize capsules.

A capsule is a small, human-reviewable unit of story possibility. It is not automatically a scene, submission, public artifact, or canon.

Support these capsule types:

- Character
- Plot or arc beat
- Scene
- Event
- Setting
- Motif
- Theme
- Question
- Freeform vision

Each capsule needs:

- Stable identifier
- Title or short label
- Human-readable body
- Type
- Author or originator
- Created and last-updated timestamps
- Visibility status, defaulting to private
- Permission context
- Source basis or a clear “manual capture” designation
- Maturity status
- Links to related capsules where practical
- Optional notes, contradictions, or open questions

The Concept Board should support:

- Create
- Edit
- View
- Archive
- Filter by type, maturity, or visibility
- Search
- Link related capsules
- Mark questions or contradictions
- Promote a selected capsule into a scene plan or draft through an explicit action

Do not automatically convert capsules into polished prose. The creator must initiate and approve any promotion.

### 3. Capsule maturity

Keep capsule maturity separate from editorial status.

Use a lightweight maturity model that a writer can understand. For example:

- Captured
- Shaping
- Ready to develop
- Developed
- Archived

A developed capsule is still private and non-canonical unless an explicit publication or editorial action changes that.

### 4. Storyworld structure

Create a simple, durable pilot-world model.

At minimum, a storyworld should include:

- Title
- Premise or orientation
- Steward or worldbuilder identity
- Visibility
- Canon policy summary
- Content notice
- Reader invitation
- Current kit or pilot version
- Selected story seed
- Scene or path collection
- Public versus private publication state

Keep the architecture ready for a future `world-manifest` record, but do not silently commit to an unresolved multi-world architecture.

### 5. Basic Reader edition

Build a reader-facing experience for approved, reader-safe content.

It must:

- Open with a story and premise, not a technical dashboard
- Render full story text cleanly
- Show title, author or contributor credit, and a short path context
- Clearly distinguish canon from alternate paths
- Avoid exposing private drafts, capsules, hidden source material, internal identifiers, or technical logs
- Offer a coherent linear reading experience
- Include an optional, simple path relationship view if it aids comprehension
- Include a clear but non-pushy invitation to contribute, marked as future or limited if contribution is not enabled
- Be accessible with semantic structure, readable typography, sufficient contrast, keyboard navigation, and reduced-motion support

Use seeded or synthetic pilot content unless the project owner explicitly provides rights-cleared material for publication.

### 6. Publishing model

Implement only a controlled publication workflow appropriate to the existing workspace.

A worldbuilder should be able to:

1. Select approved reader-safe content.
2. Preview the reader edition.
3. Confirm publication deliberately.
4. See the resulting edition status and timestamp.
5. Update selected content and create a new edition.

The publication process must not expose unpublished drafts or source material.

If the current architecture cannot safely create a durable published edition yet, implement a previewable local or controlled pilot edition and label it honestly as a preview. Do not fake a production deployment.

### 7. Provenance and permissions

For pilot content, include enough structure to demonstrate the project’s trust model.

Every reader-visible story artifact should be able to show or retain:

- Contributor or author credit
- Origin or source designation
- Visibility
- Permission context
- Canon relationship
- Publication decision
- Relevant timestamps

Start with clear, minimal metadata. Do not build a false legal system or claim legal sufficiency.

The UI must reinforce:

- Repository visibility is not permission to reuse content.
- Creative material is all rights reserved unless explicitly stated otherwise.
- No contributor is promised royalties, canon selection, adaptation rights, or commercial participation.
- Publication requires a deliberate human decision.

## Technical direction

Use the existing workspace and its established monorepo patterns. Inspect before changing architecture.

The existing repository contains a TypeScript/pnpm workspace with an Express API, PostgreSQL and Drizzle foundation, Zod validation, React/Vite frontend foundations, and Replit deployment configuration. Reuse appropriate existing packages instead of introducing competing stacks.

Preferred direction:

- Keep production frontend work in the intended web application package.
- Keep the mockup sandbox separate from production implementation.
- Use shared types and validation where the workspace already supports them.
- Make data boundaries explicit.
- Avoid putting private source material in Git history, public bundles, browser logs, or static reader output.
- Avoid secrets in source code, configuration files, process arguments, or client code.
- Preserve the existing GitHub sync convention, but do not mistake the workspace’s outbound GitHub push hook for a complete GitHub integration layer.

Before building an API or database schema for identity, contributor submissions, GitHub synchronization, or multi-world hosting, inspect the open architecture decisions. Where a decision is unresolved, label it `DECISION REQUIRED`, document the dependency, and implement only a reversible pilot-safe abstraction.

## AI and voice boundaries

The product’s long-term vision includes voice capture and optional AI assistance. They are not assumed ready for this pilot.

For Stage 0–1:

- Support manual text capture as the reliable baseline.
- You may create clearly labeled interface placeholders for future voice capture or agent assistance.
- Do not claim transcription, AI drafting, continuity review, upload ingestion, OCR, or derivative transformation is active unless it has been implemented and verified.
- Do not accept untrusted uploads.
- Do not process private or third-party creative material through an AI service.
- Do not create automatic canon decisions, automatic publication, or automatic rights determinations.

If a future-assistance placeholder appears in the interface, make it informative and honest, such as “Voice capture is planned for a later, consent-aware pilot.”

## Explicit non-goals

Do not build these features in this task:

- Public open contribution
- Public comment threads or social feeds
- Real user-upload pipeline
- OCR or file ingestion
- Real voice transcription
- Unreviewed AI writing or transformation
- CIE, PIE, or other derivative transformation tools
- Monetization, credits, royalties, revenue sharing, paid access, or adaptation marketplace features
- Legal terms that have not been owner-approved
- Full GitHub App integration
- Automatic GitHub pull-request generation for contributors
- Public community discovery network
- Moderation system beyond clear pilot boundaries
- Mobile-first native application expansion
- Multi-world platform scaling before one pilot world works end to end

These are future opportunities, not unfinished details to quietly add.

## Design direction

The product should feel like:

- A welcoming creative room for a person who does not call themselves a writer
- A careful studio for an author
- A calm library for a reader
- A trustworthy record for a steward

Avoid:

- Developer dashboards
- Generic project-management visual language
- Crypto, token, marketplace, or “community ownership” aesthetics
- Dense graph visualizations as the default reader experience
- Loud gamification
- Corporate productivity clichés
- Over-explaining every action

Prioritize:

- Generous reading space
- Clear visual hierarchy
- Thoughtful editorial typography
- Warm restraint
- Obvious privacy and publication states
- Plain language
- Small moments of reassurance
- Clear empty states that guide without patronizing
- Interfaces that work well at laptop and mobile widths

Maintain a deliberate distinction between the private Workbench and the reader-safe public edition.

## Required delivery process

1. Inspect current code, documentation, dependencies, routes, database state, and working-tree changes.
2. Summarize the confirmed implementation baseline before making large changes.
3. Create a concise implementation plan mapped to the Stage 0–1 outcome.
4. Identify architecture decisions that block irreversible work.
5. Build the smallest coherent vertical slice first:
   - Create a capsule
   - Review it in the private Workbench
   - Promote it into a simple scene
   - Select the scene for publication
   - Read it in the Reader edition
6. Validate the vertical slice before expanding features.
7. Add the worldbuilder home, organization tools, and publication experience around that working core.
8. Use realistic synthetic demo content only if needed for interface validation. Mark it as demonstration content.
9. Verify that private content cannot appear in the reader output.
10. Run the repository’s required validation commands where the environment supports them.
11. Inspect the final diff, preserve unrelated work, and report every changed file.
12. Do not commit or publish without explicit owner direction.

## Acceptance criteria

The Stage 0–1 pilot is complete only when all of the following are true:

- A worldbuilder can create, edit, organize, and archive private capsules.
- Capsules have explicit type, author, provenance basis, visibility, and maturity.
- A selected capsule can intentionally become a scene plan or draft.
- A pilot storyworld can hold a premise, stewardship context, canon policy summary, and selected content.
- A reader can read at least one coherent, approved story seed or scene in a dedicated Reader experience.
- The Reader clearly shows what is canon and what would be an alternate path.
- Private capsules and unpublished drafts do not appear in the Reader output.
- The user experience contains no ordinary-user-facing Git jargon.
- The app does not claim capabilities that are deferred or unverified.
- No untrusted uploads, public submissions, commercial promises, or real AI processing have been introduced.
- Core flows are keyboard navigable and semantically structured.
- The implementation passes available typecheck and build validation, or any limitation is reported precisely.
- The handoff includes:
  - What is confirmed working
  - What remains a mock, placeholder, or future direction
  - The current data model
  - Any `DECISION REQUIRED` items
  - Risks and safe next steps

## Definition of success

Success is not a large feature list.

Success is one person using a calm, trustworthy system to take an original idea from private capture to a deliberate, readable, attributable published story seed, without needing to understand Git and without the system making unsafe claims on their behalf.
